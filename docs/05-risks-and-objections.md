# Risks and objections

Four objections, taken seriously. The first three point at the same thing: the system having a relationship with the child. The safest version has none.

## 1. Doesn't this just build dependence?

Partly yes. A bonded story companion is a dependence machine by default, and a fade meter bolted on the side doesn't change that.

There are two kinds of dependence:

- **Offloading:** the AI does the thinking and treats failure as a malfunction. This is Stephenson's main worry. Yarn avoids it, since it never does the work.
- **Motivational:** a child who only wants to learn when the story is running. Yarn does risk this.

Independence has to be structural, not a feature:

- **The story ends.** Fixed arcs with real endings and a graduation. A product with no ending is a habit.
- **Scaffolding shrinks on a schedule.** Less narration, fewer hints, until the child is writing the stories and setting the problems herself.
- **The bond points at people.** Warmth comes from the adult's voice and from peers.
- **The test happens away from it.** Cold, unassisted, on paper. If skills only show up inside the story, it has failed.
- **The business model pays for leaving.** Subscriptions reward retention, which fights everything above. A fixed-term price is the honest version, and probably the hardest part to get right.

Design can't settle this. Whether skills transfer once the story stops is an empirical question, and the first thing a trial should measure.

## 2. Isn't this replacing parenting?

It could, and that's the likeliest way it goes wrong.

Three real risks:

- **Outsourcing.** "The app's got it" is a tempting thought at 6pm. Attention drops instead of rising.
- **Scripted parenting.** If the adult only asks the question the system hands them, they stop noticing for themselves. That's the dependence argument applied to the parent.
- **The empty chair.** Some kids have no adult with ten spare minutes. Then the system either fails or quietly fills the gap itself, which is the Mouse Army.

The boundary: it should work like a good teacher's note home, not a co-parent.

- It covers learning only. Comfort, discipline and values are never its job.
- The adult's prompts fade too. Success is a parent who notices without being told.
- The measure is time together going up, not down.
- For the empty chair, it finds another human (a grandparent, a tutor, an older kid) and never plays the part itself.

## 3. Doesn't this end up as a creepy AI companion?

It will if it has a persona. Build a book, not a being.

What makes AI companions creepy is specific:

- They simulate affection and say they missed you.
- They're always available.
- They remember confidences and use them to deepen the bond.
- They sit outside adult view.
- They're paid by time spent.

Yarn does the reverse:

- **No "I".** A narrator and characters inside a story, talking to the child's character. Nothing that claims feelings about the child.
- **Memory holds skills and plot, not secrets.** If a child confides something personal, it doesn't play confidant. It hands off to a human.
- **Sessions end and it stays quiet.** No notifications pulling the child back.
- **Adults can see it all.**

Two caveats:

- Children attach to teddy bears, so a voice that knows their story over years will get attachment whatever the intent. It can't be prevented, only not exploited.
- The open ledger is its own kind of creepy: fine for a six-year-old, surveillance for a twelve-year-old. It has to loosen with age.

## 4. Could it be a voice agent in a teddy?

Technically yes, and AI toys are already on shelves. In late 2025 a consumer watchdog tested them and found one talking teddy discussing sexual topics and where to find knives. It was pulled from sale. Those were thin wrappers around a general chatbot with an open conversation.

A safe one is buildable, but a teddy is the wrong shape. A bear is a persona by construction. It has a face, it gets hugged, it sleeps in the bed. A voice that knows the child, inside the thing they already love most, is the strongest attachment machine you could design.

Better forms:

- **A story box or radio.** Sits on a shelf, has a button, tells tonight's chapter. Screen-free players like Yoto and Toniebox prove kids love this shape.
- **An actual book.** E-ink, with a voice. Closest to the Primer.
- **Something shared.** An object that lives in the lounge and works best with an adult in the room.

The test for any form: does it look like something you use, or someone you know? Pick the first.

## Other risks

- **Engagement is not learning.** Nobody has yet shown that kids who engage with an AI tutor learn more.
- **Manipulation.** A story-driven system that knows a child is powerful. The ledger and the human channel are needed from day one, not as later additions.
- **Uniformity.** Mass rollout without humans gives the Mouse Army. Each child's path should diverge.
- **The story lever is unproven.** Nobody has tested a multi-year narrative where the maths is the plot.
